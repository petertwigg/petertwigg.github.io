*The honest assessment: what will most likely happen when the struggle is taken away.*

Part One was the hopeful half of the answer — that when the machines can do everything, the one frontier left is inward. I stand by every word of it. But it was only half, and if I stopped there I'd be doing the thing I most distrust: handing you the beautiful line and keeping the bill in my pocket. So here is the other half, the one truthfulness costs. The inward turn is one road. It is not the one most of us will take.

Start with the thing Diamandis missed, and I nearly did too. When you take survival away, you have not solved a problem — you have removed an object, and left the appetite standing. Ten thousand generations of famine, predation, and rivalry did not install in us a task that can be cancelled. They installed a hunger, and a hunger outlives the thing it was for. Switch off the need to struggle and the drive does not switch off with it. It keeps running, at full pressure, looking for ground. The question was never whether the drive survives the end of work. It does. The question is where it goes.

It has three roads. It can be transmuted — turned back and inward, refined into the fire of practice. That was Part One, and it is the road of the few. It can be turned outward — spent on the world, on frontiers or on other people; the conqueror, the empire-builder, and, when there is nothing worthy left to conquer, the man who makes an enemy for something to do. Or it can be switched off — drowned, sedated, dispersed: drink, drug, the endless feed, the engineered dream, a life lived in virtual reality in place of the real one. Escape.

Which is likeliest? Not the second. War needs scarcity — you have to be fighting over something — and abundance quietly removes the reason before it removes the restlessness. No, the road almost everyone will take is the third, because it is the one with no friction, and abundance is a friction-removing machine. Not Orwell's boot; Huxley's soma. Not the many marching on anything — the many dissolving into a screen tuned more precisely to their appetites than any emperor could have dreamed. We will not be conquered. We will be comforted into absence.

And this is not a forecast. It is a description of now. You do not need artificial general intelligence to see it — you need only look. Opioids in the towns that used to make things. Attention collapsing to the length of a swipe. Depression climbing fastest in the richest societies on earth, exactly where it "shouldn't." Abundance did not buy us contentment; it bought us better exits. The machines will simply remove the last small friction that remained — the last reason anyone had to show up to reality at all.

There is an old wheel that describes this, and it is worth naming because it feels like fate. Hard times make strong men; strong men make good times; good times make weak men; weak men make hard times. The modern epigram is G. Michael Hopf's (*Those Who Remain*, 2016) — not the ancient proverb people take it for. The ancient engine is Ibn Khaldun's (the *Muqaddimah*, 1377): the hardy solidarity that wins an age is dissolved by the luxury it wins, over three or four soft generations, and the wheel turns to the next hungry people at the gate. It is the most fatalistic idea in history, and it is fatalistic for a precise reason — it is collective and it is automatic. On the wheel there is no exit. You are simply born at your point on it and carried round.

But here is what the wheel cannot see, and it is the whole of what I want to say. Every one of those exits — the drink, the drug, the feed, and yes, even the depression — is a single move underneath. It is the flight from the emptiness that opens the instant the doing stops. Anyone who has sat down to meditate has met that emptiness; it arrives the moment the mind runs out of jobs and cannot find another. The old alchemists had a name for it — the *nigredo*, the blackening — the flat, defeated dark when the little ego discovers it has nothing left to do and no reason to exist. That darkness is not a wrong turn. It is the doorway. And the addict and the contemplative are standing at the very same mouth of it. One turns and runs for the soma. One steps in. That is the entire fork. Not inward versus outward — flee the void, or enter it.

Which is also where the wheel breaks, if it breaks at all. The cycle governs the many and it governs the generations; it does not govern the one who does the inner work. That is the oldest and least fashionable claim of every contemplative tradition, and I have come to believe it is simply true: the unit of transformation was never the species. It was always the single person. The tide of comfort-into-weakness is real, and it is real for the crowd and for the centuries — and it has never once reached the threshold of a person who chose to sit down and stay. The wheel turns. The door does not move.

So let me not sell you the hopeful line without its price. The inward path is not up against something dramatic and rare that you could brace for. It is up against something frictionless, universal, and already winning — comfort itself. It asks you to walk, on purpose, into the exact darkness that the whole of your civilisation is now engineered to spare you. That is why it will always be the few — not because the many are incapable of it, but because the exit has become too perfect, and almost no one chooses the *nigredo* when the soma is free and warm and endless.

We are gods in the making,\* carrying a predator's nervous system. Both are true at once, and which one wins is not decided by the age you happen to be born into, or by which turn of the wheel it is. It is decided, and decided again, the first time — and every time after — that you sit down, stop running, and stay.

The near-side was never only belief. It has a softer, newer twin now, and it is the harder one to cross: comfort — the frictionless exit.

Beyond belief, beyond comfort — into knowing.
