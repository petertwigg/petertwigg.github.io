There is something in you, right now, that notices the itch on the back of your arm. The same something registers the thought that pops up out of nowhere, and the emotion that bubbles up from somewhere below it — the flash of irritation, the small wave of sadness — knowing each one quietly as it arrives. That noticing is the witness, and you have never once been without it. Yet for a good many years I taught witnessing as if it were a stepping back. Find the seat behind your thoughts. Watch them drift past like weather, like traffic, like clouds crossing a wide sky. None of that is wrong. It is how most of us first loosen the grip of a mind that has been running us ragged since breakfast. But it is only the doorway, and for a long time I mistook the doorway for the room.

There is a coldness that can creep into that instruction. Watch your thoughts. Observe your feelings. Detach. Step away. Done poorly — and I did it poorly for a while — it produces a kind of inner exile. You end up hovering somewhere above your own life, arms folded, unmoved, mistaking numbness for freedom. I have met seasoned meditators who have perfected this. They are very calm. They are also, if you sit close enough to feel it, slightly absent from themselves.

The turn came when I understood who is actually doing the witnessing.

Not the personal self. Not the small, weathered "me" that wants lunch and worries about money and rehearses old arguments in the shower. That self cannot witness; it can only react. The witness is something else entirely. In the tradition I work within we call it the Ego — capital E — and I know that word carries almost the opposite meaning everywhere else, so let me be plain. I do not mean pride or self-importance. I mean the I AM. *Ego*, in Latin, means *I am*. The still, awake, unborn centre of you that was there before the personality assembled itself and will be there when it lets go. What Jung called the *Self* is not a second thing standing beside it — it is the very same reality under another name. The witness is that: the Ego, the Self, [consciousness aware of itself](/2020/05/03/what-is-consciousness/).

And here is the thing nobody told me, the thing I had to find for myself on the cushion: the Ego does not witness coldly. It witnesses with love.

I want to be careful with that word too, because *love* has been worn thin. I do not mean the warm feeling, the swell in the chest, the emotion that comes and goes with the tide of the personal self. Feelings belong to the personality; they are part of what gets witnessed, not the witness itself. The love I am pointing to is quieter and far more stable than any emotion. It is the quality of a presence that holds without grasping and allows without turning away. When the Ego witnesses grief, it does not flinch from the grief. When it witnesses fear, it does not manage the fear or fix it or narrate it. It simply stays — awake, unafraid, and somehow tender toward the whole moving weather of a human life. That staying *is* love. Not a love you generate. A love you uncover.

You can hear the difference the moment you stop confusing the two. Detachment says: this is not me, I am above it. Loving witness says: this is here, and I am here with it, and I am not going anywhere. One creates distance. The other closes it. Both look still from the outside. Only one of them is awake.

I could write another thousand words describing this and it would do you very little good. This is the part where I have to be honest with you, because it is the whole of what I teach. You cannot believe your way into this. You can nod along with every word above, agree that it is beautiful, file it under "true," and be no closer to it than when you started. Belief is cheap. It costs nothing and changes nothing. I have watched people carry beautiful spiritual ideas around for decades like furniture they never sit on.

The only currency that buys change is experiencing.

So the instruction is not: *believe* that the witness loves. The instruction is: go and find out. Sit down. Let the mind settle enough that you are no longer wholly identified with its contents. Then, instead of watching your inner life from behind cold glass, ask a different question — not "how do I detach from this?" but "who is aware of this, right now?" Turn toward the awareness itself. Rest there. Notice its quality. Notice that it is not indifferent. Notice that being witnessed in that way, even by yourself, feels like being met.

That is the experiencing. And once you have tasted it — even for three seconds, even once — you no longer have a belief about it. You have a knowing. The two could not be more different. A belief can be argued away; a hundred clever people can talk you out of it. A knowing sits underneath argument, because you were there. You felt the room, not just the doorway.

This is what I mean, always, by moving beyond belief and into knowing. Not swapping one set of ideas for a better set. Leaving the whole business of ideas behind for a while and going to look directly. Witnessing is how you learn to look. Love is what you find looking back. Experiencing is the going itself — the only part of this that was ever yours to do.

I am not asking you to take a single sentence of this on faith. I would rather you doubted all of it and then went and checked. Sit tomorrow morning, before the day gets its hands on you. Find the one who is awake. See whether it is cold — or whether it has been waiting for you with more patience than you have ever shown yourself. And if your mind is already too busy — if the witness has been swamped by the itches, the aches, the thoughts, the emotions — then say silently to yourself, [*Not this, not this*](/2026/07/14/not-this-not-this/), and keep saying it, and witness what happens within.

If you would like company on that road, the door is open: my [classes](/school-of-knowing/), and my book [*Truth Centred Meditation*](/books/), are simply longer conversations about the same three words. But the sitting is yours. It always was.
