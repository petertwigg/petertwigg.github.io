*When you set almost everything down, the road makes room for what the noise was hiding.*

I am back, with the dust barely off my boots, and I promised I would write to you from the far side of the road. So here is the first letter home.

People ask what the Camino was like, expecting, I think, to hear about the landscape, or the distance, or the blisters. All of that was real. But for me it was never really about any of it. For me it was about the people.

The sharing. The helping. The caring, the listening, the thousand small exchanges between strangers who may never meet again. And underneath it all, a life stripped back to almost nothing. You eat, you walk, you sleep. That is the whole of it. I realised very early on in the walk that the Camino gives people a small taste of what the old prayer means when it asks for things to be *on earth as it is in heaven* (Matthew 6:10). To see the light in the faces of the peregrinos, the pilgrims on the road, moved me deeply.

Something happens in that simple state. When the machinery of ordinary life falls away, when there is no inbox and no schedule and nothing to manage but the next step, the problems you had been carrying without knowing you carried them begin to loosen their grip. And in the space they leave, revelations become possible: things about yourself, about your life, that the noise had been hiding. The road makes room for them.

Along the way I had my share of the usual aches and pains, and they gave me an unexpected chance to practise. There is an old contemplative method called **neti neti**, "not this, not this," which I have written about before, in [Not This, Not This](/2026/07/14/not-this-not-this/). It belongs to meditation, where it is used to quiet a chattering mind by setting down, one by one, everything you are not. On the Camino I turned it on my body. When a pain announced itself I would meet it with the same patient refusal: *not this, not this.* Partly to ease the pain, and partly to show myself, again, the illusion that the mind and the ego build around a mere sensation.

A pain rose up in the back of my left knee, and with repeated application of neti neti I was able to release it. I will be honest with you, because there is little point in these letters if they are not honest. It did not always work. I also picked up a shin splint in the same leg, and that one rather got the better of me. The pain would lift, then come roaring back with noise and a fanfare. The method is true. I am not always equal to it. That, too, is worth knowing.

The road showed me something else, and this one shook me. Over the days I watched myself playing out the attributes of my own sign in the Chinese zodiac, the Dog. It is one thing to know, in the abstract, that you are a Horse, or a Rat, or a Dragon. It is another thing entirely to catch yourself living out the pattern in real time, to feel the archetype moving you from the inside. It was revelatory and it was confronting, in equal measure. The road does that. It holds up a mirror you did not pack.

A great deal of your attention on the Camino goes to the body. You are forever caring for it, nursing it, cajoling it along. It gets easier as you grow fitter, but the balance between what the body can give and the will to keep going is a fine one, and it is so easy to give up. Suffering on the Camino is simply the natural outcome of what you ask of yourself: blisters, shin splints, sprains, the occasional fall, cuts and bruises, and, for some of us, the plain fact of our age.

And yet. I met a pilgrim woman of eighty-nine. There are people in their seventies and eighties tramping steadily along that road in great numbers. You pass, too, the small shrines where someone did not make it: a cross, a photograph, a bench with a name carved into it, flowers, and stones, some written on and some left plain, each one quietly honouring the life of a peregrino who died doing this very thing. You walk on a little more quietly after each one.

Here is what I felt in myself, and heard echoed by nearly everyone I spoke to. However hard the day had been, you would wake the next morning and all but spring out of bed to begin again. Anyone who knows me knows I am not a morning person. Yet I came to love setting out along the trail at six or seven o'clock in pitch darkness, waiting for a dawn that comes late in the Spanish autumn, the country keeping its clocks an hour ahead of the sun. Walking into the dark, trusting that the light would come. It always did.

That, in the end, is what the road gives you. Not an argument. Not a better opinion about life. A taste, in the body and in the company of others, of how simple and how luminous living becomes when you set almost everything down. The pilgrims have known it for a thousand years. I know a little more of it now than I did when I left.

Beyond belief, into knowing. Some things you can only find out on foot.
