*When the machines can do everything, the last frontier is inward.*

An essay came my way this week that is worth your time. Peter Diamandis — the futurist, founder of the XPRIZE — sits down with the question almost nobody in the technology world will hold still for: when artificial intelligence can do any cognitive task better than any human, and robots any physical task better than any human, what is a human life for? Not economically — that has policy answers. Existentially. What gives a life meaning when there is nothing a person must do that a machine cannot do better? If you'd like the full context of what I'm responding to, his piece is linked at the end, and it's a generous, serious thing. He is asking the right question. I want to take it seriously, because it is the same question I have lived inside for forty years — only approached from the other side of a line.

Diamandis reaches back to a Greek word, and he is right to. *Skholé* — the root of our "school" and "scholar" — did not mean lessons. It meant the freedom from labour that the survival-work of others bought for you, and what you did with it: philosophy, art, contemplation, the examined life. He calls the coming age the democratisation of *skholé*, and that is exactly right. For the first time in history, the cleared ground that once belonged only to aristocrats could belong to everyone.

Here is where I part company with him — gently, and where I think the whole matter turns.

He asks what we will do with that freedom, and answers with ten things to do. Curate. Explore. Compete. Create. Patronise. He is not wrong that these are good; they are. But notice that every one of them is still an activity. He has taken the freedom from work and filled it straight back up with better work. The calendar is nobler. It is still a calendar. And I suspect that most people handed infinite leisure and a menu of worthy pastimes would do with it what the Romans did with theirs — not philosophy and the Olympics, but bread and circuses, now delivered through a screen tuned more precisely to our appetites than any emperor could have dreamed. Diamandis sees the danger. He names the Roman fork himself. But when he asks what decides which way we go, his answer is a single word — culture — and there he stops. That blank is the most important sentence in his essay, and he leaves it empty.

I want to fill it in, because the tradition I teach from has always known what goes there.

Free time does not become flourishing on its own, and the reason is this: the deepest human things are not activities at all. They are not things you do. They are states you enter — ways of being you become. You cannot reach them by adding more to the calendar, however noble the additions. You reach them by a discipline that runs in the opposite direction: inward, back toward the one who is doing all the doing.

This is the distinction the whole matter rests on. Everything on Diamandis's list — curating, exploring, creating — lives on the near side of a line: the world of doing and having. It is a magnificent near side; I am not against it. But there is a far side, and no amount of activity reaches it, because it is not made of activity. It is made of direct knowing — of turning attention back on consciousness itself and discovering, first-hand, what you actually are underneath all the doing. That crossing is what meditation is for. Not relaxation. Not one more wellness item to slot between the tennis and the wine collection. It is the single discipline that answers the question Diamandis opened and could not close — because it is the only one that operates on the far side of the line.

Take his own example: the athlete, the body pushed to its limit for the joy of it — "the body as instrument." He is closer than he knows. The body is an instrument. But an instrument for what? He means performance. The contemplative traditions mean something else entirely — the body as the finely tuned instrument through which a subtler perception becomes possible; the instrument of reception, not of achievement. When survival no longer needs the body, and performance no longer needs it either, that older use of it is still there, waiting, and almost no one now remembers it is there at all.

So my answer to Diamandis is not a longer list. It is a change of verb. The question is not what will humans *do* when the machines can do everything. Humans will do plenty; we always have. The real question — the one his most thoughtful readers were already reaching for in his own comment section — is what will humans *become*. When every outer frontier has been crossed by something faster and stronger than us, when the ocean floor is mapped and Mars is walked and every image already painted, one frontier remains that no machine can cross on your behalf, for the simple reason that the whole point of it is that it must be crossed first-hand. The frontier inward. The knowing of what you are.

That is not a consolation prize for a species made obsolete. It is the thing the obsolescence finally clears the ground for. For ten thousand years we have told ourselves we would attend to the soul once the harvest was in. The harvest is about to be permanently in. The oldest excuse we have is gone. And what remains, when it goes, is the question we have been deferring since we first came down from the trees. It was never *what will you do*.

We are gods in the making. It was always *who will you be when there is nothing left to hide behind*.

That is the work. It always was.

Beyond Belief — Into Knowing.
