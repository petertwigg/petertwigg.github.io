*Pistis into gnosis — the one move the whole path is about.*

There's a question I sometimes put to people who tell me they believe in something — God, the soul, the light, whatever their word for it is. I ask, gently: do you believe it, or do you know it? Almost always there's a pause. And in that pause they feel the thing this whole essay is about — that believing and knowing are not the same, and that nearly everything they've been handed sits on the near side of that line.

Take something ordinary. You can believe a stove is hot because there's a sign on it, or you can know it because you touched it. You can believe you are loved because you were told, or you can know it in a way that needs no telling. The belief and the knowing may use the very same words. In the body they are worlds apart. One you could be talked out of. The other you could not.

The Greeks had two words for this, and the whole of what I teach lives in the gap between them.

The first is *pistis* — faith, belief, trust. It's the assent you give to what you've been told: the creed you can recite, the doctrine you'd defend, the proposition you hold. Pistis is real, and it is not nothing. But it is second-hand. It travels by report. You believe because someone you trusted said so, because it was written down, because the people around you hold it too.

The second word is *gnosis* — knowledge, but not the kind you carry out of a library. Greek, like French and German, keeps two meanings of "to know" apart: there is knowing *about* a thing — *savoir*, *wissen* — and there is knowing a thing by direct acquaintance — *connaître*, *kennen*. Gnosis is the second kind. It is not information about the divine. It is the tasting of it. You no longer trust that the honey is sweet because the jar says so; it is on your tongue.

Here is something the tradition is clear about, and we have quietly forgotten: belief was never rated highest. When Plato drew his famous map of the mind — the divided line — he set *pistis* down in the lower half, the realm of opinion, the flickering confidence we have about passing physical things *(Republic, 509d–511e)*. Above it he placed reasoning, and above that, at the summit, *noesis*: direct intellectual seeing, knowing by immediate apprehension. Belief was a rung you were meant to climb *off*. The top of the ladder was a kind of sight.

The word *gnosis* itself became a banner. The Gnostics of the early Christian centuries — much maligned, often deservedly, but right about this one thing — insisted that what saves is not believing the correct doctrines but *knowing*, first-hand, who and what you actually are *(Irenaeus, Against Heresies, sets out their pistis-versus-gnosis divide)*. And even the subtlest teachers inside the orthodox fold saw the force of it without throwing faith away. Clement of Alexandria called *pistis* the foundation and *gnosis* the building raised upon it: faith was the doorway, and knowing was the house you came to live in *(Clement, Stromateis)*. That is the honest version, and the one I hold. Belief is not stupidity. Belief is the trailhead. The error is to make camp at the trailhead and call it the mountain.

This is exactly where the Western stream I teach from takes the thread up again. Rudolf Steiner built a whole path on the claim that the higher worlds are not objects of belief but of *cognition* — that spiritual perception can be schooled the way the eye is schooled. And Carl Jung, asked near the end of his life, on television, whether he believed in God, gave the answer that could hang over my door. He paused, and said: *"I don't need to believe. I know."* *(Jung, BBC "Face to Face," 1959)*. Not arrogance — its opposite. He had *met* the thing. And once you have met it, belief becomes as unnecessary as faith that the sun has risen while you stand in the daylight.

I labour the difference because our age has mislaid it. We hold more information about spirituality than any people who ever lived, and less direct knowing than almost any. We read, we listen, we gather beliefs, we take sides — and we mistake the growing heap of *pistis* for progress, mostly without noticing we're doing it. Nothing in history has been better engineered to keep the heap growing than the screen in your hand. I didn't notice it either — I didn't know the trap from the inside; I hadn't a clue. It was only by living the experiences within myself, over years, that I came to realise, and then to know.

Because no one is ever argued into gnosis. No book hands it over — nor any essay, this one included. It arrives one way only: you sit down, you go quiet, you stop rehearsing what you believe, and you begin — clumsily, then less clumsily — to look for yourself. Meditation is not a technique for calming down. It is the laboratory where a belief is brought to the fire of direct experience, and either dies or blossoms.

That is the whole reason my school carries the name it does. Not the School of Belief. The School of *Knowing*. The aim was never to hand you a better set of things to hold; it was to walk you to the place where the holding can be set down, and you can *see*.

And something is waiting to be seen. When belief finally ripens into knowing, what it comes to know is not an abstraction — it is a light, particular and buried, already alight in you. That is where this goes next: the divine spark, the thing gnosis actually finds when it arrives. But that is the following essay. For now it is enough to feel the line — and to notice, honestly, which side of it you have been standing on.

Beyond belief — into knowing. That was always the only journey.
